# What Do Income Distribution Curves Look Like? A Few Examples

Most executives have heard of income distribution, but usually do not know what the actual curves look like.

This is not surprising because there not all that many such curves easily available. Instead, the focus is on the 1 percent, the Gini coefficient, and other shorthand's for income distribution.

Here we show four curves with the absolute (in dollars) incomes as a function of population for four cities. We cap the maximum at the 99% percentile since the curve goes to infinity,

We chose the cities for the following reasons.

- Bogotá as an example of a city with high income inequality. It is interesting to note that the top 1% of people make about the same amount of money as Stockholm, even though average income levels are much lower.
- Stockholm has compressed income levels as is seen in the income scale only reaching 3 times the median income while Bogotá reaches 8 times.
- London is one of the cities in Europe with the largest upper and middle classes.
- New York can be seen as a global benchmark for income distribution. Note how high the mean and median incomes are compared to the three other cities.

What to note in the graphs:
1. The horizontal axes shows the percent of population ordered from lowest to highest income
2. The vertical axes show the income level as a multiple of the median income in respective city. Multiply this number by the indicated median income and you get the absolute income per capita in PPP$.
3. Mean and median incomes per capita are indicated, as are the income levels at the 99% of the population cutoff.
There are other ways to present rthe vertical axes, but we chose this way because it shows the differences in the curves.
